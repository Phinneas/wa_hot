Here's the honest version most guides won't give you. The Washington side of the Columbia River Gorge has exactly three hot springs worth your time, and they all draw from the same underground source. One is a century-old bathhouse. One is a free wild soak you can only reach a few months a year. One is a modern Japanese-inspired retreat. That's the whole list. No hidden fourth spring, no secret pools. Just three genuinely different ways to soak in the same remarkable water.

I dug into the geology and the current access for each one. Here's what you actually need to know.

## The water they all share

Before the three springs, one fact that ties them together. All of it comes from the Wind River geothermal system, heated deep below the Gorge.

And that water is a bit of an oddball. According to USGS surveys, the spring here discharges a slightly saline sodium-chloride water. That makes it the lone exception among Washington's thermal springs, which are otherwise a different chemical type entirely. Chemically, this water has more in common with the hot springs down in the Oregon Cascades than with its neighbors to the north. So when you soak here, you're in the one corner of Washington where the water breaks the state's own pattern.

Now, the three ways to experience it.

## 1. Carson Hot Springs Resort — the historic bathhouse

This is the anchor, and the one with the longest story. Mineral water was first found on the bank of the Wind River back in 1876, and the resort era took hold by the early 1900s.

The heart of it is a 1930s bathhouse where individual clawfoot tubs draw geothermal water straight from the nearby Wind River. It's a no-frills, vintage experience by design. You can take a private individual tub and set your own temperature, or use the larger public soaking pools. There's a cold pool too, if you like alternating hot and cold.

You can soak by the hour during set times, or get access by booking an overnight stay. If you want privacy, some rooms come with their own balcony tub.

Go here if you want history and a soak that feels unchanged by time. Our [Carson Hot Springs visitor guide](/blog/carson-hot-springs-complete-visitor-guide) has the full details.

One heads-up that has nothing to do with the spring itself. There's a completely separate Carson Hot Springs in Carson City, Nevada. If you're searching online, make sure you're looking at the Washington one.

## 2. Wind River Hot Springs — the free wild soak

Also called The Springs at Carson, this is the primitive, no-cost option. And it's the one that takes real planning.

You get two natural bedrock pools right on the Wind River, sitting somewhere around 102 to 105 degrees. There are no facilities of any kind. It's inside the Columbia River Gorge National Scenic Area, so pack out everything you bring, and no camping.

Here's the catch that trips people up. Reaching the pools requires crossing the river. That's only safe when the water is low, which typically means late summer, roughly July through September. In spring and early summer, snowmelt raises the Wind River to levels that make crossing genuinely dangerous. Do not force it.

There's also a history of private-property and access friction around the approach, so tread carefully and respect posted boundaries.

Go here if you want a free, wild, earn-it soak and you're willing to time your visit to the river. Our [Wind River Hot Springs guide](/blog/wind-river-hot-springs) walks through the access options in detail.

## 3. Tenzen Springs & Cabins — the modern onsen

The newest of the three, and the most different in feel. Tenzen opened in June 2022 on a 100-acre property near Stevenson. The owner, Nanae Yoshida, built it around Japanese onsen bathing culture, and it's the first of its kind in the Pacific Northwest.

Each cabin has its own private two-person geothermal soaking tub, fed by water drawn from about 3,000 feet below the Wind River. You control the temperature yourself. The water flows continuously through the tub, and there's a real sustainability angle here. After each stay, the water is filtered and reinjected into the aquifer through a well so it can reheat naturally.

A few things to know before you book. It's adults only. There is no day use at all, so access is for overnight cabin guests only. It runs year-round, which makes it a strong winter option, and check-in is contactless with no staff on site. Recent nightly rates started around $312 on weeknights and $414 on weekends.

Go here if you want privacy, design, and a quiet retreat, and you don't mind that soaking comes bundled with a cabin stay.

## Which one is right for you

Simple way to choose.

Want history and a classic bathhouse soak you can do by the hour? Carson.

Want a free, wild soak and you can time it for low water in late summer? Wind River.

Want a private, modern, design-forward retreat for an overnight? Tenzen.

Three springs, one ancient source, three completely different experiences. That's the Washington side of the Gorge, honestly told.
