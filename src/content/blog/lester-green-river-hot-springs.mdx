Here's a story the "best hot springs" lists will never tell you. Deep in the Cascades, along the upper Green River, there once sat a hot springs resort town with a sanatorium, two doctors, and a couple hundred residents. Today you cannot go there. Not because it's a hard hike or a washed-out road, but because it's illegal. The whole valley is locked behind a gate, and if you try to reach it, you can be ticketed. This is the story of the springs Washington sealed off, and the woman who spent her life refusing to leave.

One quick clarification first. If you came looking for "Liberty Hot Springs," there isn't one. Liberty in Washington is a mountain, a famous climbing route, and an old gold-mining ghost town near Cle Elum. No hot spring. The real story here is Lester and Green River Hot Springs, two lost settlements in the same forbidden valley.

## A valley full of vanished towns

The upper Green River valley used to be alive. It formed the western approach to Stampede Pass, and the railroad filled it with small towns. Weston. Lester. Green River Hot Springs. Nagrom. Maywood. Humphreys. Eagle Gorge. Names most people have never heard, because they're all gone now.

Two of them were built around hot water.

Green River Hot Springs, properly named, was home to a large sanatorium built around the natural springs. By 1907 and 1908 the settlement had around 225 residents and two doctors. People came for the water and the promise of healing. Then the sanatorium burned down, and the town began its long fade.

A couple of miles away sat Lester, founded in 1892 by the Northern Pacific Railway. Lester existed to serve the steam locomotives climbing over Stampede Pass. At its peak it had a hotel, a roundhouse, a post office, a general store, and a school. By 1908 it held about 250 people.

Both towns lived and died by the railroad and the water. And both ended up on the wrong side of a decision made in Tacoma.

## Why the gate went up

The Green River is where Tacoma gets its drinking water. It has been the city's primary water supply since 1913.

To protect that supply, Tacoma began buying up the valley. The city started purchasing property in Lester in 1963, and by 1969 it owned the land the town sat on. The goal was watershed security, keeping people and contamination out of the source of a city's water.

That's a reasonable public-health aim. It also meant the slow eviction of an entire community and the permanent closure of a beautiful stretch of the Cascades.

Today the upper valley is a gated municipal watershed, closed to public access. Washington Trails Association puts it plainly. The area is closed, and visitors may be ticketed. The closure has been a source of tension with hikers and recreation advocates for decades, but the gate has held.

## The Battle of the Lester Gate

People did not go quietly.

When Tacoma moved to block access, it led to protests. At one point the gates on the only all-weather road into Lester were destroyed, an incident that got its own name: the Battle of the Lester Gate. It was a small fight in the scale of Washington history, but a real one. A city's need for clean water on one side, a community's right to its own home on the other.

The city won. But not before one resident turned refusal into a decades-long stand.

## Gertrude Murphy, the last resident

Gertrude Murphy was a schoolteacher in Lester. As the town emptied around her, she became its champion.

By 1980 only 26 people were left. Murphy served on the school board and fought to keep Lester alive, even pushing to get it recognized on Washington's historic register. When the state closed the school in 1985, deciding the town was simply too small to justify it, she worked full time trying to save what remained.

The valley kept taking from her. In 1993 her home burned down. She moved about a mile and a half out of town, into a cabin she wryly called "the suburb of Lester." She stayed until the end. Gertrude Murphy died in 2002, the last resident of a town that no longer officially existed.

## What's left, and why you still can't go

Tacoma finished the job in 2017, demolishing the last standing buildings in Lester: a guard house, a gas and oil shack, and a warehouse. Some foundations and small traces remain. The hot springs are still out there in the closed valley, geologically real, quietly flowing, and completely off-limits.

I want to be clear about this part, because it matters. I'm not going to give you directions, coordinates, or a workaround. The watershed is closed for a legitimate reason, the closure is actively enforced, and trespassing can get you ticketed. This is a place to understand, not to visit.

That's the strange fate of the Green River hot springs. They outlasted the sanatorium, the railroad, the town, and the last resident. They're still here. And they belong now to a city's water supply, not to us.

If you want a Washington hot spring you can actually soak in, [look elsewhere in the Cascades](/blog/best-hot-springs-near-seattle-washington). This one is a story, not a destination. Sometimes that has to be enough.
