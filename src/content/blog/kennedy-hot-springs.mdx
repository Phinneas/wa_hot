<p>If you've found an old guidebook or forum thread raving about Kennedy Hot Springs, I need to give you the bad news up front: the spring no longer exists. A massive flood buried it under five feet of rock and debris in October 2003, and the U.S. Forest Service has stated plainly that there are no plans to bring it back.</p><p>But the story is worth telling—and worth knowing before you plan a trip. Kennedy was one of only three hot springs on the flanks of Glacier Peak, and for decades it was the most loved backcountry soak in Washington. Here's what it was, what happened to it, and where you can soak instead.</p>

<h2 id="what-kennedy-hot-springs-was">What Kennedy Hot Springs Was</h2><p>Kennedy Hot Springs sat at about 3,600 feet in the Glacier Peak Wilderness, at the confluence of Kennedy Creek and the White Chuck River in the Mt. Baker-Snoqualmie National Forest, roughly 21 miles from Darrington. It was one of three geothermal springs on Glacier Peak, alongside Gamma and Sulphur Creek hot springs.</p><p>The water was gentle by hot spring standards. A 1961 USGS survey measured the source at about 86°F—right around body temperature—with a sodium-chloride-bicarbonate chemistry flowing at 3 to 5 gallons per minute into a log tub. It was a "wilderness spa" in the truest sense: a warm pool in deep old-growth forest, reached by a 5.2-mile hike up the White Chuck River Trail from the end of White Chuck Road, off the Mountain Loop Highway.</p><p>The site had history beyond the water. A Forest Service guard station built in 1927 stood near the springs, and generations of hikers, climbers bound for Glacier Peak, and horse packers used Kennedy as a waypoint and a reward.</p>

<h2 id="the-2003-flood">The Flood That Buried It: October 2003</h2><p>Between October 20 and 22, 2003, an extraordinary storm dropped up to 10 inches of rain in 24 hours near Glacier Peak. The White Chuck River—and debris coming off the volcano's slopes—overwhelmed the valley. Kennedy Hot Springs was buried under five feet of mud, rock, and woody debris. The 1927 guard station was destroyed, along with the bridges, corrals, and shelters at the site.</p><p>The damage extended far beyond the springs. The White Chuck River Trail was largely destroyed—by 2006, less than one-third of it remained—and the storm caused an estimated $12 million in damage across the region's trail system. The Forest Service's assessment of Kennedy has never wavered: the springs were buried, and there are no plans to excavate or restore them.</p>

<h2 id="can-you-visit-today">Can You Visit Today?</h2><p>This is where old and new information collide, so let me be precise.</p><h3 id="the-site-yes-the-soak-no">The site, yes. The soak, no.</h3><ul><li>White Chuck Road has long since reopened, and determined hikers do reach the Kennedy Hot Springs area via rough alternate routes, including approaches over Meadow Mountain</li><li>These are expert-level trips: unmaintained routes, river crossings, route-finding, and serious backcountry commitment in a remote valley</li><li>Hikers who have made the trip—including a first-hand account from 2020—report no visible evidence of the hot springs at the site</li><li>No credible recent report describes any soakable water at Kennedy. Sites that currently list it as "open" and "soakable" are recycling pre-2003 data</li></ul><p>If you're an experienced off-trail hiker drawn by the history and the Glacier Peak backcountry, the journey can be worthwhile on its own terms. If you're going for a soak, you will be disappointed.</p>

<h2 id="why-the-spring-wont-be-restored">Why the Spring Won't Be Restored</h2><p>Kennedy sits in designated wilderness, where the Forest Service's mandate is to let natural processes play out—not to rebuild amenities. Excavating a buried spring with heavy equipment would be both ecologically damaging and legally fraught under wilderness rules. Geothermal systems can shift and re-emerge over decades on their own, but nothing in two decades of reports suggests that has happened at Kennedy.</p>

<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2><p>Kennedy Hot Springs belongs to Washington's history now. For nearly a century it was the rare backcountry soak that ordinary hikers could reach, and the October 2003 flood took it in a single weekend. The valley is still there, still wild, and still visitable by the very determined—but the warm water under the old-growth canopy is gone. Honor it by choosing one of the living springs above, and by treating them gently enough that they outlast us.</p><p>You can find Kennedy Hot Springs, marked as destroyed, on our <a href="/springs/kennedy-hot-springs">hot springs directory page</a>.</p>
