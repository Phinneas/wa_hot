<p>Wind River Hot Springs might be the most rewarding soak in the Columbia River Gorge—and the hardest one to reach legally. Two natural bedrock pools sit right at the edge of the Wind River near Carson, Washington, fed by geothermal water that bubbles up through the riverbed itself. The catch: the river swallows the pools for most of the year, and private property surrounds the banks.</p><p>I think of Wind River as a spring for planners. Time your visit for low water, understand the access situation before you go, and you'll likely have one of Washington's prettiest primitive soaks nearly to yourself.</p>

<h2 id="about-wind-river-hot-springs">About Wind River Hot Springs</h2><p>Wind River Hot Springs sits along the lower Wind River just outside Carson in Skamania County, on the Washington side of the Columbia River Gorge. The springs are not marked on USGS maps, which has kept them a locals' secret for decades.</p><p>The springs occupy a curious legal niche: they sit below the high-water line of the Wind River, which makes them public land under Washington law even though private property rings the riverbanks. That same quirk means the pools spend most of the year underwater. They typically emerge only from late July through mid-September, when the river drops to its late-summer low.</p><p>When exposed, the springs form two bedrock pools. Hot water seeps up through the bottom of the larger pool at around 106–107°F, keeping it near 105°F with room for four or five soakers. The smaller pool runs a couple of degrees cooler, around 102°F, and fits about three people. In late summer, visitors sometimes build small sand-and-gravel mixing pools along the streambed, blending hot spring water with cold river water. The cold Wind River beside you makes natural hot-and-cold contrast therapy easy.</p>

<h2 id="location-and-accessibility">Location and Accessibility</h2><p>The springs lie near the mouth of the Wind River, a few minutes from the town of Carson and about an hour from Portland. Getting to the river is easy. Getting to the pools legally is the challenge.</p><h3 id="the-access-situation">The Access Situation</h3><ul><li>The private landowners adjacent to the springs no longer allow parking or foot access across their property, and vehicles parked along nearby private roads risk being towed</li><li>A footbridge near the springs sits in a legal gray area—visitors have reported citations for using it, so treat it as off-limits</li><li>The legal approaches are to wade or swim across the Wind River at low water, or to walk up the rocky streambed from a lawful access point, staying below the high-water line</li><li>Both approaches are only realistic in late summer when the river runs low; the current is cold and swift, so judge conditions honestly before crossing</li><li>Never park on private land or posted roads, and never cross fenced or posted property to reach the river</li></ul><h3 id="when-to-go">When to Go</h3><ul><li>Late July through mid-September is the window—before that, the pools are submerged</li><li>Weekdays are quiet; expect a few locals and fishermen on summer weekends and during fishing season</li><li>Check recent river conditions before making the drive; a wet summer can delay the season</li></ul>

<h2 id="facilities-and-amenities">Facilities and Amenities</h2><p>There are none at the springs themselves—no toilets, no trash service, no changing rooms, no marked trail. This is a fully primitive site, so pack in everything you need and pack out everything you bring.</p><p>Camping is not permitted at the springs. A small private campground, Wind Wood Rivers, operates on the adjacent property with a handful of primitive sites. And if the river is running too high to reach the pools, the historic Carson Hot Springs Resort—the old Saint Martin's Hot Springs—is just minutes away with a paid bathhouse.</p>

<h3 id="rules-and-etiquette">Rules and Etiquette</h3><p>Everything at Wind River comes down to respect. Stay below the high-water line, which keeps you on public land. Do not park on or cross private property, and do not use the contested footbridge. No camping at the pools, no glass, no soap in the water, and pack out every scrap of trash. The springs survive because they sit in a legal gray zone—behavior that angers the neighboring landowners or draws enforcement attention is exactly how places like this get shut down for good.</p>

<h2 id="tips-for-first-time-visitors">Tips for First-Time Visitors</h2><h3 id="what-to-bring">What to Bring</h3><ul><li>Sturdy water shoes or sandals with grip for the rocky streambed and river crossing</li><li>A dry bag for your clothes, towel, and phone if you plan to wade or swim across</li><li>Water and snacks—nothing is available at the site</li><li>A trash bag for packing everything out</li><li>A swimsuit if you prefer one; expect a relaxed, clothing-optional atmosphere at quiet times</li></ul><h3 id="safety-and-considerations">Safety and Considerations</h3><ul><li>The Wind River is cold and moves fast even at low water—if the crossing looks beyond your ability, it is; come back another day or soak at Carson Hot Springs Resort instead</li><li>Test pool temperatures before climbing in; the source water emerges hot enough to scald</li><li>Watch for slippery algae on the bedrock around the pools</li><li>Leave no trace, and give fishermen and other soakers plenty of space</li></ul>

<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2><p>Wind River Hot Springs asks more of you than almost any other soak in the Gorge: the right season, the right river level, and a careful approach that respects the private land around it. Meet those conditions and you get two perfect bedrock pools, a cold river for contrast plunges, and a good chance of having it all to yourself on a weekday morning.</p><p>You can also find Wind River Hot Springs on our <a href="/springs/wind-river-hot-springs">hot springs directory page</a> along with other Washington soaks worth planning a trip around.</p>
