Washington's hot springs aren't random. Every one of them is a readout of what's happening deep beneath the Cascades, and once you understand the geology, you can look at any spring in the state and know roughly what's feeding it. I built this guide from a single authoritative source, a 1982 USGS survey that measured and mapped the chemistry of Washington's thermal springs. Here's what it reveals about why hot water reaches the surface here, and where.

## The one-sentence answer

Washington has hot springs because it sits on a volcanic arc. Magma beneath the Cascade Range heats groundwater, and faults give that water a path back to the surface.

That's the short version. The interesting part is that not all of Washington's hot springs are heated the same way, and the chemistry proves it.

## Two families of springs

The USGS found that Washington's thermal waters split into two clear types, and the split tells you about the rock below.

The first family is dilute and alkaline, a sodium-bicarbonate water. These springs are associated with granite. [Olympic](/blog/olympic-hot-springs-guide) and [Sol Duc](/blog/sol-duc-hot-springs) belong here. The water hasn't reacted much with the surrounding rock, which shows up in its chemistry as a very small oxygen-isotope shift.

The second family is charged with carbon dioxide, a saltier sodium-bicarbonate-chloride water. These issue on or near the big stratovolcanoes. [Baker](/blog/baker-hot-springs-washington-state), [Gamma](/blog/gamma-hot-springs), [Ohanapecosh](/blog/ohanapecosh-hot-springs), and [Kennedy](/blog/kennedy-hot-springs) are in this group. Their water has reacted extensively with rock at depth, and it carries a heavy load of CO₂.

One spring refuses to fit either box. [St. Martin's](/blog/carson-hot-springs-complete-visitor-guide), the source at Carson in the Columbia Gorge, is the only sodium-chloride thermal water in the state. Chemically it looks more like the hot springs of the Oregon Cascades than like anything else in Washington.

## Where the heat comes from

The Cascade Range holds five young volcanoes: Mount Baker, Glacier Peak, Mount Rainier, Mount Adams, and Mount St. Helens. These are the engine.

The USGS survey measured how hot the underground reservoirs feeding each spring likely are, and a few stand out. Gamma Hot Springs, in the Glacier Peak Wilderness, showed the highest estimates, with methods pointing anywhere from roughly 157°C to over 200°C at depth. Baker Hot Springs sits above a reservoir estimated around 150°C, and the survey flagged it as the one system in the state with real geothermal development potential. Ohanapecosh, on Mount Rainier's flank, most likely draws from an aquifer near 110°C.

Most of Washington's thermal springs cluster near or just west of the Cascade crest, exactly where you'd expect if volcanism is the heat source. The exceptions prove the rule. A few warm and mineral springs appear out in the Columbia Basin and near Walla Walla, in a different geological setting entirely, and Olympic and Sol Duc sit off in the Olympic Mountains rather than the Cascades.

## The carbon dioxide mystery

Here's the detail that makes Washington's volcanic springs genuinely unusual.

The CO₂-charged springs put out far more carbon dioxide than their measured temperatures can explain. The gas escaping at Gamma is over 99 percent CO₂. At Baker and Sulphur it runs in the 95 to 97 percent range. That volume doesn't match the heat.

The USGS conclusion is striking. The carbon dioxide likely originates deep, in the lower crust or the upper mantle, then dissolves into water circulating far below the surface before rising. In other words, the fizz in these springs is closer to deep-earth gas than to anything happening near the surface. That's not something you can say about most hot springs anywhere. I wrote about what this means for actually visiting these springs in [Washington's hottest hot springs](/blog/washingtons-hottest-hot-springs).

You can even read the difference in the isotopes. Carbon-13 in the hot CO₂-charged springs runs more depleted, around −10 to −12 parts per thousand, than in the cold soda springs scattered through the Cascades. And only the hot springs actively build travertine, the pale mineral crust left behind as they deposit calcium carbonate.

## How to read any Washington spring

Put it together and you have a simple framework.

If a spring is dilute, alkaline, and near granite, it's the quieter type, groundwater warmed without dramatic deep chemistry. Olympic and Sol Duc.

If it's saltier, fizzing with CO₂, and sitting near a stratovolcano, you're looking at water that traveled deep, reacted with rock, and carried mantle gas back up with it. Baker, Gamma, Ohanapecosh.

And if it's the lone sodium-chloride oddball in the Gorge, that's St. Martin's, chemically an Oregon spring that happens to surface in Washington.

That's the geological map of Washington's hot springs. Not a list of places to soak, but an explanation of why the hot water is there at all, drawn straight from the science.
