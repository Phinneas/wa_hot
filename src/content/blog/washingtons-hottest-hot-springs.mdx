Here's the thing nobody tells you in the "10 Best Washington Hot Springs" posts. The state's most geologically remarkable thermal springs are also the ones you're least likely to ever reach. The chemistry that makes them extraordinary is tied to the same wild, volcanic, flood-prone terrain that keeps them locked away.

I went looking for the real story in a 1982 USGS study that most travel writers never open. What I found reframed how I think about every hot spring in the Cascades.

## The bubbles are older than the mountains

Most people assume a hot spring is just groundwater that got warm near a volcano. For a lot of Washington springs, that's roughly true. But a whole family of them does something stranger.

They come out charged with carbon dioxide. A lot of it. [Gamma Hot Springs](/blog/gamma-hot-springs) discharges gas that is over 99 percent CO₂. Sulphur and Baker are in the same range.

The USGS looked at the temperatures of these springs and reached a blunt conclusion. The water simply isn't hot enough to generate that much CO₂ on its own. So where's it coming from? Their answer points down. Way down. The gas likely originates in the lower crust or the upper mantle, then dissolves into water that circulates deep before rising to the surface.

Sit with that for a second. When you'd stand at one of these springs, the fizz reaching your skin started its journey below the crust. It's deep-earth gas, not surface froth.

## Every spring has a fingerprint

Washington's thermal springs split into two families, and you can tell them apart by chemistry.

The first group is dilute and alkaline, associated with granite. [Olympic](/blog/olympic-hot-springs-guide) and [Sol Duc](/blog/sol-duc-hot-springs) belong here. The second group is the CO₂-charged type, and these cluster near the big stratovolcanoes: Baker, Rainier, Glacier Peak, Adams, and St. Helens.

The tell is in the isotopes. Carbon-13 in the hot CO₂-charged springs runs noticeably more depleted, around −10 to −12 parts per thousand, than in the cold soda springs scattered through the Cascades. Only the hot ones actively build travertine, the pale mineral crust that marks a spring that's been depositing calcium carbonate for a long time.

You don't need a lab to appreciate the point. These aren't interchangeable puddles of warm water. Each one is a readout of what's happening miles beneath it.

## The four hottest systems in the state

Four CO₂-charged springs sit on top of the hottest estimated aquifers Washington has.

**Gamma Hot Springs** is the extreme case. It surfaces at 65°C, roughly 149°F. But the deep reservoir feeding it may run far hotter, with estimates stretching from 157°C to over 200°C depending on the method. It's the closest thing Washington has to a serious geothermal system.

**Baker Hot Springs** surfaces near 44°C, about 111°F, sitting above an aquifer the USGS pegged around 150°C. Of every spring in the study, this was the one they flagged as having actual development potential.

**[Ohanapecosh Hot Springs](/blog/ohanapecosh-hot-springs)** comes up at 48°C, roughly 118°F, from a travertine mound, with a most-likely reservoir temperature near 110°C.

**[Sulphur Hot Springs](/springs/sulphur-creek-hot-springs)** is cooler at the surface, around 35°C, but still tied to a reservoir estimated between 110 and 117°C.

For contrast, consider Olympic Hot Springs. Its escaping gas is mostly argon and methane, with almost no CO₂. It's a completely different animal, and the chemistry proves it. The fingerprint is real.

## Now the part the listicles skip

You've met the four most impressive thermal systems in Washington. Here's how many you can actually visit.

One.

**[Kennedy Hot Springs](/blog/kennedy-hot-springs)** was once a popular destination. A 2003 flood buried it. It's gone, and the site is inaccessible.

**Gamma and Sulphur** sit deep in the Glacier Peak Wilderness, with no maintained trail to soak at either. As I write this, that whole corridor is worse than usual. The Miners Fire ignited in the wilderness in mid-August 2026, and the Forest Service closed the Pacific Crest Trail from Suiattle Pass south to Mica Lake, the exact approach to Gamma. That order runs until rescinded, so check the live alert before you plan anything.

**Ohanapecosh** is inside Mount Rainier National Park. It's a protected thermal feature, not a soaking spot. The 1982 study even noted the irony: some of the highest-temperature systems in the state are precisely the ones locked inside wilderness and national parks, unavailable for any use.

See the pattern? Flood, wilderness, national park. The same forces that make these springs geologically dramatic are the ones that put them out of reach.

## The one you can actually touch

That leaves [Baker Hot Springs](/blog/baker-hot-springs-washington-state).

It's reachable by a rough forest road and a short walk, and it's genuinely popular. Washington Trails Association lists more than 50 trip reports for it. If you want to experience a CO₂-charged Washington spring with deep-earth gas rising through the water, this is the realistic one.

A favor to everyone, though. Because it's the accessible member of an otherwise unreachable family, it takes a beating. Pack out what you bring in. Keep it soakable for the next person.

## Why any of this matters

The generic guides tell you Washington has hot springs. True, but shallow. The deeper story is that its most remarkable thermal systems are scientific landmarks first and soaking spots only rarely. They're windows into what's moving beneath the Cascades, and most of them are sealed shut by flood, wilderness, and law.

The next time you see one on a "best of" list, you'll know which question actually matters. Not "how do I get there," but "what is this water telling me about the earth it came from."
