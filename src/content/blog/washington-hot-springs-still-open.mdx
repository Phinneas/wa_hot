Not every hot spring you read about is one you can still visit. Some are thriving resorts. Some are wild pools you can still reach on foot. And some are gone, buried by floods, sealed behind water-supply gates, or protected inside national parks. Before you plan a trip around a spring you saw online, here's an honest status check on Washington's springs, past and present, and the stories behind the ones you can no longer reach.

## The survivors: still open today

**[Sol Duc Hot Springs Resort](/blog/sol-duc-hot-springs)** is the Peninsula's easy, developed option, and it has deep roots. The name comes from a Quileute word for sparkling waters, and the springs were used by the Quileute and Makah peoples long before settlers arrived in the 1880s. A hotel was built at the site in 1912. Today it's a concession-run resort with three mineral pools running roughly 98 to 104°F, open seasonally from late March into October.

**[Carson Hot Springs Resort](/blog/carson-hot-springs-complete-visitor-guide)**, in the Columbia River Gorge, is the historic bathhouse that never closed. Mineral water was found on the bank of the Wind River in 1876, and the resort era followed. Its 1930s bathhouse still draws geothermal water into individual clawfoot tubs. It runs year-round.

**[Baker Hot Springs](/blog/baker-hot-springs-washington-state)**, near Mount Baker, remains a popular wild soak reached by a rough road and a short trail. No frills, but open and well-visited.

## Still there, but harder than they used to be

**[Olympic Hot Springs](/blog/olympic-hot-springs-access)** is the cautionary tale of access. These natural pools in the Elwha Valley were once an easy visit. Then the Olympic Hot Springs Road washed out, and it has stayed closed to vehicles at Madison Falls for years. Reaching the springs now means roughly a 10-mile trek one way, on foot or bike, plus a final walk-in trail. The pools still flow, but the National Park Service warns they're unmaintained, untested, and may carry harmful bacteria. Still there. Just no longer easy.

**[Wind River Hot Springs](/blog/wind-river-hot-springs)**, the free primitive pools in the Gorge, are seasonal by nature. You can only reach them safely when the river is low enough to cross, typically late summer. The rest of the year, the water makes it too dangerous.

## Gone, or off-limits for good

This is where the history turns.

**[Kennedy Hot Springs](/blog/kennedy-hot-springs)**, once a popular destination in the Glacier Peak Wilderness, was buried in a 2003 flood. It's simply gone, and the site is inaccessible.

**[Ohanapecosh Hot Springs](/blog/ohanapecosh-hot-springs)** sits inside Mount Rainier National Park. It's a protected thermal feature now, not a soaking spot. You can appreciate it, but you cannot use it.

**[Green River Hot Springs and Lester](/blog/lester-green-river-hot-springs)** tell the most dramatic closure story in the state. The upper Green River valley once held a string of small railroad towns, and two were built around hot water. Green River Hot Springs had a sanatorium and, by 1907, around 225 residents and two doctors. Lester, founded in 1892, served the steam locomotives crossing Stampede Pass.

Then Tacoma needed the water. The Green River has been the city's primary water supply since 1913, and to protect it, Tacoma bought up the valley, acquiring Lester by 1969. The closure was fought, including an incident known as the Battle of the Lester Gate, when protesters destroyed the gates blocking the road. The city prevailed. The last resident, schoolteacher Gertrude Murphy, held on until her death in 2002, and Tacoma demolished the final buildings in 2017.

Today the whole valley is a closed municipal watershed. The springs still flow somewhere in there, but the area is off-limits and enforced, and visitors can be ticketed. I'm not giving directions to it. This one is a story, not a destination.

## The honest status summary

If you want a soak you can count on: Sol Duc, Carson, or Baker.

If you're willing to work for it and accept the risk: Olympic Hot Springs, or Wind River in late summer.

If you're chasing history rather than a soak: Kennedy, Green River, and Lester are the ghosts, reachable now only in the record, not on the ground.

Washington's hot springs map isn't fixed. Roads wash out, watersheds close, floods rewrite the backcountry. Before any trip, check the current agency page for the spring you want, because "open" here has a way of changing.
